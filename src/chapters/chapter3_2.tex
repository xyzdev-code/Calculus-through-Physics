\section{Introduction to derivatives}


Finally, right before we begin on calculus proper, there are a few more equations you will need to know. The \emph{electric force} on a charge \(q_1\) between two charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) is\footnote{Why does this law hold though? Well, it is derived from experiments.}:
\begin{equation}
  \vec{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{\rc^2}\hrc 
\end{equation}


I shall use \(\brc\) to indicate the separation vector between the two charges, so if \(\vec{r_1}\) is the position of \(q_1\) and \(\vec{r_2}\) is the one of \(q_2\), then \(\brc=\vec{r_1}-\vec{r_2}\). \(\hrc\) will indicate the separation vector having itself divided by its magnitude to obtain a basis vector, a process we call \emph{normalization}. \(\rc\) will indicate the magnitude of \(\brc\), or the \emph{distance} between the two charges. The constant \(\epsilon_0\) is called the \emph{permittivity of free space} which is around \(8.8542\times 10^{-12}\frac{\si{\coulomb}}{\si{\newton \cdot \metre^2}}\). You can verify the units cancel out correctly yourself. Due to the square in the denominator, laws like this are called \emph{inverse square laws}. For a set of charges, we can apply the \emph{superposition principle} to find the net force acting on any single one of them. Suppose we have several point charges \(q_1,q_2,\ldots,q_n\) and want to find the net force acting on \(q_1\). If we have \(F_2,\ldots,F_n\) being the individual forces exerted on \(q_1\) by \(q_2,\ldots,q_n\), then the net force on \(q_1\) will be:
\begin{equation}
  F = F_2 + F_3 + \ldots + F_n
\end{equation}


\emph{Potentials} are one of the most important constructs we are going to use, it is related to \emph{potential energy}. If a charge \(q\) has an electric potential energy \(U\), then \(U=qV\) where \(V\) is the potential. In other words, it is the electric potential energy per unit of electric charge. It bears the unit of \emph{volt}. With this, we will now tackle a famous law, the \textbf{Child-Langmuir law}. 


Imagine two parallel metal plates separated by a gap of vacuum with a distance \(d\) along the x-axis. Electrons are "boiled" off one of the hot metal plates at \(x=0\), held at a potential of \(0\si{\volt}\). The electrons accelerate across the gap to the other plate at \(x=d\), held at a positive potential \(V_0\), meaning any charge \(q\) travelling from the prior plate to this plate will gain \(qV_0\) worth of electric potential energy. If we allow this process to continue for a while, a steady current \(\vec{I}\) will be formed between the plates.\footnote{For the purposes of this text, we will assume the plates are large relative to the separation between the plates, so \emph{edge effects} can be ignored.}

We will let \(V(x)\) be the potential at any point along the axis, therefore \(V(0)=0\) and \(V(d)=V_0\). \(v(x)\) shall be the velocity of the electron at some \(x\). Lastly, we will define \emph{volume charge density}, which is simply the amount of electric charge per unit volume, as \(\rho(x)\).

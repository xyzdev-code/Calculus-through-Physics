
  \section{Cross products and triple products}
The previous section perfectly segues into this section. \textit{Cross products} is an operation on vectors that happens solely in the third dimension. Do not confuse it with the dot product, both uses different operators and should not be used interchangeably. Firstly, we must recall that one of the basis vectors is \(\khat\) and it is one unit in the z direction.

The cross product is a vector, whose magnitude is the area of a parallelogram formed by the two operands (which are vectors). \marginnote{\footnotesize \textit{The operands of \(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\) are \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\)}.} More formally, the cross product is defined as:
\begin{definition}{Cross product}{}
  The \textbf{cross product} is an operation on two vectors and the result is another vector which is perpendicular to both vectors. It is defined by the formula:
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{a}\times\vec{b} = (||\vec{a}||)(||\vec{b}||)\sin(\theta)\vec{n}
  \end{equation*}
  where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) and \(\vec{n}\) is the unit vector perpendicular to both of the operands such that all three vectors obey the "right hand rule"\sidenotemark. The common way to compute the cross product is using the following formula:
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{a}\times\vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix}
      \ihat & \jhat & \khat \\
      \vec{a}_x & \vec{a}_y & \vec{a}_z \\
      \vec{b}_x & \vec{b}_y & \vec{b}_z \\
    \end{vmatrix}
  \end{equation}
  Lastly, do not confuse \(\vec{n}\) with \(\khat\) as both vectors are sometimes not the same.
\end{definition}
\sidenotetext{\footnotesize \textit{The right hand rule states that if you use your right hand and point the index finger in the direction of \(\vec{a}\), your middle finger in the direction of \(\vec{b}\)}, your thumb should be pointing in the direction of \(\vec{n}\).} 

The cross product is very ubiquitous in physics and vector calculus alike. 
\begin{example}{Calculating cross products}
  Given that \(\vec{a}=\begin{bsmallmatrix}
    1 \\
    2 \\
    0 
  \end{bsmallmatrix} \) and \(\vec{b}=\begin{bsmallmatrix}
    -2 \\
    1 \\
    0
  \end{bsmallmatrix} \), then \(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\) would be:
  \begin{align*}
    \vec{a}\times\vec{b} &= \begin{vmatrix}
      \ihat & \jhat & \khat \\
      1 & 2 & 0 \\
      -2 & 1 & 0
    \end{vmatrix} \\
                         &= 0\ihat - 0\jhat + 5\khat \\
                         &= 5\khat
  \end{align*}
  \begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
      \draw[line width=2pt, -stealth, red] (0,0,0) -- (1,0,-2) node[pos=1.3]{\(\vec{a}\)};
      \draw[line width=2pt, -stealth, blue] (0,0,0) -- (-2,0,-1) node[pos=1.3]{\(\vec{b}\)};
      \draw[line width=2pt, -stealth, purple] (0,0,0) -- (0,5,0) node[pos=1.05]{\(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\)};
      \draw[line width=2pt, -stealth, gray] (0,0,0) -- (0,1,0) node[anchor=west]{\(\vec{n}\)};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{Figure of two vectors and their cross product.}
  \end{figure}
\end{example}


An important fact to note is that the operation is not commutative. In fact, the operation is called \textit{anti-commutative}. This means that \(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}=-\vec{b}\times\vec{a}\). In addition, if the angle between the operands is any integer multiple of \(\pi\), then the result is \(\vec{0}\). This should be trivially seen from the formula for the cross product. The derivation for the cross product is also easy, given two vectors \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) and a angle \(\theta\) between them, simply use the sine rule and the result should be obvious. The cross product has many real world applications.
\section*{Application: angular momentum}
\begin{example}{Torque}{}
  Torque is a force that is common in our daily lives, such as the turning of a doorknob or the turning of a wrench. The formula for torque is \(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F}\), where \(\vec{\tau}\) is the torque, \(\vec{r}\) is the distance from the pivot point to the point where the force is applied and \(\vec{F}\) is the force applied. The direction of the torque can be determined by the right hand rule.
\end{example}

\Cref{figtripvec} is the example of triple vector multiplication. You would represent it as \(\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c})\). The result here is simply the volume of the parallelepiped formed in \Cref{figtripvec}.
\begin{definition}{Triple vector product}{}
  The \textbf{triple vector product} is a product of three 3 dimensional vectors. The most common of which, is the scalar triple product which is \Cref{figtripvec}. The formula for which is \(\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})\), which yields the volume of the parallelepiped with the sides being the three vectors. Another type of triple vector multiplication is vector triple product. It is simply \(\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})\). We can use \textbf{Lagrange's formula} to compute it easily: \(\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = \vec{b}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c}) - \vec{c}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\).
\end{definition}
\begin{example}{Volume of parallelepiped}{}
  We shall derive the formula for the volume of a parallelepiped given its height, breadth and length. We shall denote them \(h\), \(b\) and \(l\) respectively. Notice that we define the sides of the parallelepiped like this:
  \begin{align*}
    \vec{a} &= \begin{bmatrix}
      l \\
      0 \\
      0
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \vec{b} &= \begin{bmatrix}
      0 \\
      b \\
      0
    \end{bmatrix} \\
      \vec{c} &= \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        0 \\
        h
      \end{bmatrix}
  \end{align*}
  Now, we use the triple vector multiplication formula. However, take note of the brackets and the order of operations: 
  \begin{align}
    \vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c} ) &= \vec{a} \cdot \begin{vmatrix}
      \ihat & \jhat & \khat \\
      0 & b & 0 \\
      0 & 0 & h
    \end{vmatrix} \nonumber \\
                                            &= \begin{bmatrix}
                                              l \\
                                              0 \\
                                              0
                                            \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}
                                              bh \\
                                              0 \\
                                              0
                                            \end{bmatrix} \nonumber \\
                                            &= lbh
                                            \label{volofpara}
  \end{align}
  The result in \Cref{volofpara} is trivial and should come as no surprise to the reader.
\end{example}


As for vector triple product, the process of computation is very similar. We first evaluate the cross product of the two vectors in parenthesis, then take the cross product of the first and resultant vector from the previous computation. We shall give a simple proof for Lagrange's formula.
\begin{theorem}{Lagrange's formula}{}
  Given \(\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}\), show that \(\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = \vec{b}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})-\vec{c}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\).
\end{theorem}
\marginnote{\footnotesize\textit{A sidenote here, when it is written \(\vec{a}\vec{b}\), this is not the cross or dot product, instead it is equal to \(\begin{bsmallmatrix}
  a_x b_x \\
  a_y b_y \\
  \dots
\end{bsmallmatrix} \).}}
\begin{proof}{Lagrange's formula}{}
  We will start by evaluating \(\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})\):
  \begin{align}
    \vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) &= \vec{a}\times\begin{vmatrix}
      \ihat & \jhat & \khat \\
      b_x & b_y & b_z \\
      c_x & c_y & c_z
    \end{vmatrix} \nonumber \\
                                        &= \vec{a}\times\begin{bmatrix}
                                          b_yc_z - b_zc_y \\
                                          b_zc_x - b_xc_z \\
                                          b_xc_y - b_yc_x
                                        \end{bmatrix}  \nonumber \\
                                        &= \begin{vmatrix}
                                          \ihat & \jhat & \khat \\
                                          a_x & a_y & a_z \\
                                          b_yc_z - b_zc_y & b_zc_x - b_xc_z &  b_xc_y - b_yc_x
                                        \end{vmatrix} \nonumber \\
                                        &= \begin{bmatrix}
                                          a_y(b_xc_y - b_yc_x) - a_z(b_zc_x - b_xc_z) \\
                                          \dots
                                        \end{bmatrix} \nonumber \\
                                        &= \begin{bmatrix}
                                          a_yb_xc_y + a_zb_xc_z - a_yb_yc_x - a_zb_zc_x \\
                                          \dots
                                        \end{bmatrix}
                                        \label{lagrangeformtrip}
  \end{align}
  We will examine the \(\ihat\) component of \ref{lagrangeformtrip}, the other two components are similar. Then, notice that:
  \begin{align}
    \vec{b}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c}) - \vec{c}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}) &= \vec{b}(a_xc_x+a_yc_y+a_zc_z) - \vec{c}(a_xb_x+ a_yb_y+ a_zb_z) \nonumber \\
                                                                &= \begin{bmatrix}
                                                                  b_x(a_xc_x+a_yc_y+a_zc_z) \\
                                                                  b_y(a_xc_x+a_yc_y+a_zc_z) \\
                                                                  b_z(a_xc_x+a_yc_y+a_zc_z)
                                                                \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}
                                                                  c_x(a_xb_x+ a_yb_y+ a_zb_z) \\
                                                                  c_y(a_xb_x+ a_yb_y+ a_zb_z) \\
                                                                  c_z(a_xb_x+ a_yb_y+ a_zb_z)
                                                                \end{bmatrix} \nonumber \\
                                                                &= \begin{bmatrix}
                                                                  a_yb_xc_y + a_zb_xc_z - a_yb_yc_x - a_zb_zc_x \\
                                                                  \dots
                                                                \end{bmatrix}
                                                                \label{lagrangeformtripscalar}
  \end{align}
  As you can see, the \(\ihat\) components of \ref{lagrangeformtrip} and \ref{lagrangeformtripscalar} are identical. Hence, we have shown Lagrange's formula.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}{}{}
  The above proof is a tedious pedagogical exercise mainly created to drill in the concepts of the cross products.
\end{remark}

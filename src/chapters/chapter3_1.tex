\chapter{Derivatives}
\section{Essence of calculus}


Perhaps framing an established pedagogical subject like calculus through the unconventional lens of physics is not optimal, but I will try my best. One of the most infamous problems in physics is the Brachistochrone curve, which has a deceptively simple problem statement:
\begin{problem}
  \textit{(Brachistochrone curve)} Find the curve that connects a point A and a lower point B, where B is not directly below A, on which a ball slides frictionlessly under the influence of a uniform gravitational field from A to B in the shortest time.\footnote{Uniform gravitational field means that no matter the position of the ball, the gravitational force acting on the ball is the same. Additionally, note that the initial velocity of the ball is taken to be 0.}
\end{problem}


What do you think is the fastest path? Perhaps a straight line, a quarter circle? Turns out, it is more complex than that. It was said that when Johann Bernoulli posed this question to the scientific journal Acta Eruditorum, Newton stayed up for an entire night and mailed in the solution anonymously. Bernoulli immediately recognized it was from Newton, exclaiming that he "recognizes a lion from his claw mark". This problem was introduced while calculus was in its infancy, so we are able to tackle it without the advanced calculus machinery. Unfortunately, the whole solution would require integration, the major topic in Part III. I shall first present a sketch of what is in store. Some preliminaries out of the gate, the formulas for kinetic energy (KE) and gravitational potential energy (GPE) are as such:


\begin{align}
  KE &= \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \\ 
  GPE &= mgy
\end{align}


where m is the mass of the ball, v is the velocity of the ball and y is the height of the ball above ground. These constitute the total energy of the ball, which by the conservation of energy, must stay constant. Thus, we have

\begin{align}
  KE_{initial} + GPE_{initial} = KE_{final} + GPE_{final}
\end{align}

or, simplifying 

\begin{equation}
  gD = \frac{1}{2}v^2
\end{equation}


where D is the height from B to A and v is the final velocity. Turns out, if we do some manipulation, we can show that the path follows the law of reflection for light, which then enables us to derive 

\begin{equation}
  dx = \sqrt{\frac{y}{D-y}}dy
  \label{cycloid}
\end{equation}


which is known as a \emph{differential equation} whose solution is a shape called a \emph{cycloid}. This is the answer to our original problem.

The core of deriving \Cref{cycloid} is by analyzing the small displacements of the ball as it moves down, tiny displacements, or \emph{infinitesimal} displacements. Infinitesimal refers to a quantity that is smaller than any positive real number, but not zero, which sounds weird at first. Remember this sentence, this implies that infinitesimals are \textit{not} real numbers. Calculus is the study of infinitesimal changes, and through analyzing small changes, we can understand a much larger picture. It turns out, a large class of problems in physics and even other sciences can be solved by the methods of calculus. It is surprisingly powerful how we can essentially utilize tiny changes to understand the whole. But enough of philosophical musings, let's get to the math. You would notice that the entirety of this part (and the next part too) revolves heavily around the Brachistochrone problem, needless to say, you should get yourself familiar with the problem. As a teaser, the rigor in calculus is due to the \emph{limit}, and I shall present a crude but intuitive definition of it. 


\begin{definition}
  The \textbf{limit} of a function \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) is the value that \(f(x)\) approaches as \(x\) gets arbitrarily close to \(a\). We write
  \begin{equation}
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L
  \end{equation}
  where \(L\) is the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\). \footnotemark
\end{definition}
\footnotetext{Actually, \(f(a)\) does not even have to exist, it can be undefined.}


Instead of plunging into evaluating limits immediately, I want to motivate the reader to understand the origin of the need for limits. Take the following function as an example \footnote{This is actually the \(\sinc\) function, used commonly in signal processing.}
\begin{equation}
  f(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{x}
\end{equation}


I am about to pose a completely nonsensical question, what is \(f(0)\)? Easy, inasmuch as there is a 0 in the denominator, \(f(0)\) is undefined. That is not wrong, but perhaps when we plot the graph of \(f\), we shall notice something interesting

  \centering

      \addplot[mark=none, color=blue]{sin(x)/x};
      \addplot[holdot] coordinates{(0,1)};
    \end{axis}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Plot of \(f(x)\).}
\end{figure}


Supposing you are the first one to notice that \(f\) seems to approach 1 as x goes to 0. You would be absolutely correct. Turns out, the limit as \(x\) approaches 0 is 1, which we write as
\begin{equation}
  \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin(x)}{x}=1
  \label{sinc0}
\end{equation}


Using limits, we are able to "eliminate" this discontinuity in the function. Limits enable us to study behavior of functions that otherwise would not be possible by conventional means. Of course, I have not shown why this limit is 1 or that it even \textit{exists}. While the exact derivation will be left to the later chapters, we can use SageMath to help us evaluate this limit. To put it simply, Sage is a mathematics software system like Mathematica, but unlike many of its counterparts, it is free and open source. To evaluate this limit in Sage, we use the following syntax (enter the following code line by line):
\begin{sagecommandline}
  sage: f(x)=sin(x)/x
  sage: f.limit(x=0)
\end{sagecommandline}

Line 1 defines the function and line 2 evaluates the limit of \(f\) as \(x\) approaches 0. Expectedly, Sage yields the value 1 for the limit. We shall be using Sage for the rest of the book so do read up on the syntax of Sage. A quick Google search should suffice. Coming back to calculus, treat limits as an integral part (pun intended) of calculus, but the nitty-gritty shall be brushed aside for now. All in all, remembering that infinitesimals like \(x\) in \Cref{sinc0} are not real numbers, but are special quantities that enable us to reason, with rigor in due time, a lot of problems.

\section{Differentiation basics}

Onto the calculus portion that is very much anticipated. No more the quick skimping over precalculus topics. We shall enter this discussion with a very simple question that will yield many interesting results. Take a look at the graph below which describes the displacement of particle \(x\) over time \(t\) \sidenote{\footnotesize \textit{x starts at 0, because if I were to plot the graph with negative values of x, it does not make sense physically. Unfortunately, this makes the cubic nature of the graph less obvious.}}:


\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \begin{axis}[xlabel=\(t\), ylabel=\(x\), axis lines=left, domain=0:4]
      \addplot[mark=none, color=blue]{x^3 -2*x +1};
    \end{axis}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Graph of \(f(t)=t^3-2t+1\).}
  \label{cubgraph}
\end{figure}


From \Cref{cubgraph}, we see that this is a cubic graph. It should not to be hard to see that the graph is always changing value. This corresponds to the fact that the velocity of the particle is not constant. Also, it should not be difficult to see that the rate of change of the graph is not constant. This, meanwhile, shows that the acceleration of the particle is not constant. We can use the gradient formula to verify this:
\begin{definition}{Gradient}{}
  The gradient of a (straight) line passing through two points \((x,f(x))\) and \((x+a, f(x+a))\) is:
  \begin{equation}
    \textit{Gradient} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x+a)-f(x)}{a}
  \label{gradient}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}
 

In case you have forgotten, the rate of change of displacement with respect to time is velocity, and the rate of change of velocity with respect to time is acceleration. Hence, we can use the gradient formula to find the average velocity of the particle over a certain time interval. For example, if we want to find the average velocity between \(t=1\) and \(t=2\), we can use \Cref{gradient}:
\begin{align*}
  \textit{Average velocity} &= \frac{f(2)-f(1)}{2-1} \\
  &= \frac{(2^3-2\cdot2+1)-(1^3-2\cdot1+1)}{1} \\
  &= \frac{5}{1} \\
  &= 5
\end{align*}


Using \Cref{gradient} again, we can pick some points on the graph. You would notice that if you plug in different points then the gradient is different. The main, central question is: if the rate of change is not constant, then what is the rate of change at any single point along the function? This is where the gradient formula fails. Our current formula can only give us the average velocity for some time period, however, we want something that gives us the gradient of the graph at every single point along the graph. In physics, this is analogous to the velocity of the particle at every single point along its journey, or its \textit{instantaneous velocity}. The forefathers of calculus were all brilliant and they noticed a special fact that applies to many functions. Let us zoom in to the graph where \(t=2\), we want to zoom in as closely as we can.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \begin{axis}[xlabel=\(t\), ylabel=\(x\), axis lines=left, xticklabels={2, \(2+h\)}, xtick={1,2}, yticklabels={\(f(2)\), \(f(2+h)\)}, ytick={1,2}]
      \addplot[mark=none, color=blue]{x};
    \end{axis}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Zoomed in graph of \Cref{cubgraph}.}
  \label{zoomin}
\end{figure}


In \Cref{zoomin}, we realize that the graph, when zoomed in sufficiently, essentially is a straight line. This is exciting, we can use our gradient formula which works on straight lines. In addition, on the x-axis, we mark \(2+h\), a small distance away from 2. We can infer that \(h\) is a really tiny number, almost going to 0 as we keep zooming in. We use the limit notation to denote this. Hence, we can denote it as \(\lim_{h\to0}\). \marginnote{\footnotesize \textit{The limit notation and its concepts will be discussed in a later chapter, for now, just keep in mind that this means \(h\) is a really tiny number, almost 0. A way I like to interpret this is that if some \(\epsilon \to 0\) (epsilon approaching 0), then for any \(N>0\), \(\epsilon<N\).}} You may be itching to use the gradient formula now. We can substitute in the values:
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h}
\end{align*}


However, notice that we have to divide throughout by \(h\), and \(h\) is very small, infinitesimally small. Some careless manipulation and you would say the expression blows up to infinity. What the formula does tell us, though, is the gradient practically at 2. This gives us an idea. Can we just replace 2 with any value of \(x\)? This brings us to the definition of the \textit{derivative}.
\begin{definition}{Derivative}{}
  The \textbf{derivative} of a function \(f(x)\) is the instantaneous rate of change of that function. It can be written as \(f'(x)\), \(\frac{df(x)}{dx}\) or \(\dot f\).\sidenotemark  
  \begin{equation}
    f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}
\sidenotetext{\footnotesize \textit{Sometimes, to save writing a few letters, the inputs to the functions may be omitted, such as \(f\) instead of \(f(x)\). This is more common when the function takes in multiple inputs.} }


\begin{challenge}{Alternative form of the derivative}{}
  Instead of considering the slope between \(x\) and \(x+h\), what if we consider the slope between \(x\) and \(x-h\). What would the derivative formula become?
\end{challenge}

In the definition of the derivative, we can see that the derivative is also a function. Also, the definition states that it is the "instantaneous rate of change". This simply means that we are looking at the rate of change of the function over a very, very tiny range of \(x\). Of course, the definition may feel contradictory. How can some value change "instantaneously"? Well, this is resolved by not exactly by substituting \(\Delta x=0\), instead we let it "approach" 0. As a result, using this clever little mathematical trick, we avoided this problem. However, some of you may have noticed we have not evaluated any derivatives, we just defined them. It is no good to not answer our original question. To illustrate a derivation of a derivative, we shall use a much simpler graph, \(f(x)=x^2\).
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \begin{axis}[xlabel=\(x\), ylabel=\(y\), axis lines=left, domain=-2.5:2.5]
      \addplot[mark=none, color=blue]{x^2};
    \end{axis}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Figure of \(y=x^2\).}
  \label{quadgraph}
\end{figure}


From \Cref{quadgraph}, we can consider what happens at \(x=2\). We shall use the definition of the derivatives, which is also more commonly referred to as "first principles".
\begin{example}{Derivative of \(f(x)=x^2\)}{}
  To find the derivative at \(x=2\), we use the first principles as such \sidenotemark:
  \begin{align*}
    f'(2) &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(2+h)^2-4}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{4+4h+h^2-4}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0} 4+h \\
    &= 4
  \end{align*}

  To find the derivative of \(f\), we use a similar manipulation:
  \begin{align*}
    f'(x) &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0} 2x + h \\
    &= 2x
  \end{align*}
\end{example}
\sidenotetext{\footnotesize \textit{We can ignore \(h\) in the second last step because as a general rule of thumb, as long as substituting \(h=0\) does not create an expression of \(\frac{a}{0}\), we can let \(h=0\). Of course, this is very not rigorous and there are other exceptions that will be very evident after the reader has read the later chapter of limits. }}

In other words, we have also found the instantaneous rate of change of \(f(x)=x^2\). We shall solve the problem we posed at the start of the chapter, what is the instantaneous rate of change or velocity for \Cref{cubgraph}.
\begin{example}{Derivative of \(f(t)=t^3-2t+1\)}{}
  Using the first principles, we have:
  \begin{align*}
    f'(t) &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(t+h)-f(t)}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(t+h)^3-2(t+h)+1-(t^3-2t+1)}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{t^3+3t^2h+3th^2+h^3-2t-2h+1-t^3+2t-1}{h} \\
    &= \lim_{h\to0} 3t^2 + 3th + h^2 - 2 \\
    &= 3t^2 - 2
  \end{align*}
  Therefore, the instantaneous velocity of the particle at any time \(t\) is \(3t^2-2\). For example, at \(t=1\), the instantaneous velocity is simply \(1\), and at \(t=2\), the instantaneous velocity is \(10\). Remember how we found the average velocity between \(t=1\) and \(t=2\) to be 5? Notice how the instantaneous velocity at \(t=1\) is less than 5, and at \(t=2\) is greater than 5. By looking at the graph, we can see that this makes sense. The value at \(f(1)\) is smaller than the value of \(f(2)\), hence the average velocity is greater than the instantaneous velocity at \(t=1\). Similarly, the instantaneous velocity at \(t=2\) is greater than the average velocity.
\end{example}


Then, we shall touch on something that is very similar to the derivative, the tangent line. The tangent line is a straight line that just touches the curve at one point. The gradient of the tangent line is equal to the gradient of the curve at that point. This means that if we know the derivative of a function at some point, we can find the equation of the tangent line at that point. Remember, the derivative is the instantaneous rate of change of the function. You will find the equation of the tangent line to be very useful in many instances. The exact derivation will be left to Problem \ref{tangentlineprob}, but given a function \(f(x)\), the equation of the tangent line at \(x=a\) is:
\begin{definition}{Tangent line}{}
  Given a differentiable function \(f(x)\), the tangent line to the function at \(x=a\) is:
\begin{equation}
  y = f'(a)(x-a) + f(a)
\end{equation}
\end{definition}


\subsection{Newton's method}


  Newton's method is a method to approximate the roots of a function. It is based on the idea that if we have a guess \(x_0\) that is close to the root, then we can use the tangent line at \(x_0\) to find a better approximation \(x_1\). The equation of the tangent line at \(x_0\) is:
  \begin{equation}
    y = f'(x_0)(x-x_0) + f(x_0)
  \end{equation}


  To find the root of the tangent line, we set \(y=0\):
  \begin{align*}
    0 &= f'(x_0)(x-x_0) + f(x_0) \\
    -f(x_0) &= f'(x_0)(x-x_0) \\
    x-x_0 &= -\frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)} \\
    x &= x_0 - \frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)}
  \end{align*}


  Therefore, we can use the formula:
  \begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}
  \end{equation}



  We shall use the above to find a better approximation of the root. We can repeat this process until we reach a satisfactory level of accuracy. Take the function \(f(x)=x^2-x-1\) as an example. We want to find the root of this function. We can start with an initial guess of \(x_0=1\). The derivative of the function is \(f'(x)=2x-1\). We can use Newton's method to find a better approximation:
  \begin{align*}
    x_1 &= x_0 - \frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)} \\
    &= 1 - \frac{1^2-1-1}{2\cdot1-1} \\
    &= 2
  \end{align*}



  Repeating the process:
  \begin{align*}
    x_2 &= x_1 - \frac{f(x_1)}{f'(x_1)} \\
    &= 2 - \frac{2^2-2-1}{2\cdot2-1} \\
    &= \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.6667
  \end{align*}



  Continuing:
  \begin{align*}
    x_3 &= x_2 - \frac{f(x_2)}{f'(x_2)} \\
    &= \frac{5}{3} - \frac{\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^2 - \frac{5}{3} - 1}{2\cdot\frac{5}{3}-1} \\
    &= \frac{13}{8} = 1.625
  \end{align*}


  And again (finally):
  \begin{align*}
    x_4 &= x_3 - \frac{f(x_3)}{f'(x_3)} \\
    &= \frac{13}{8} - \frac{\left(\frac{13}{8}\right)^2 - \frac{13}{8} - 1}{2\cdot\frac{13}{8}-1} \\
    &= \frac{21}{13} \approx 1.6154
  \end{align*}



  If you were wondering what is the exact solution to the polynomial, it is in fact the golden ratio \(\phi\), which is about 1.618, very close to our fourth iteration (about \(99.8\si{\percent}\) accurate). We will cover some cases where this method may not converge in Problem \ref{newtonquad}.


I would like to comment that normally, if not explicitly stated in the question to use "first principles", you can use the derivative rules you will learn in the next chapter to solve derivatives much more quickly and efficiently. In all honesty, using the "first principles" is very tedious and generally would not be used. There are also times where the function is not differentiable at all. Consider the Dirichlet function defined back in Chapter 1, it is not differentiable at any point. However, this is a very contrived example and you will not (usually) encounter such functions in real life. 



The final elephant in the room is the notation of derivatives. There are many different notations for derivatives, and they are all used in different contexts. The most common notations are \(f'(x)\), \(\odv{f}{x}\), and \(\dot f\). The first notation is used when we want to denote the derivative of a function. The second notation is used when we want to denote the derivative of a function with respect to a variable \(x\) explicitly. It would be a shame if I did not tell you the joke about dividing d in \(\odv{}{x}\). Basically, some students might misinterpret the \(d\) in the fraction as a variable. Instead, you should consider \(\odv{}{x}\) as a whole, not a fraction (even though after you learn the chain rule you might sort of disagree). Mathematically, it is an \textit{operator}. An operator is a sort of "function" that takes a function and maps it to a different function. Unlike functions however, an operator \(\hat{Q}\) acting on a function \(f(x)\) is written as \(\hat{Q}f(x)\).\sidenote{\footnotesize \textit{We shall denote operators as non-bolded characters with a hat to differentiate them from the unit vectors.}} Going back to the derivative, I believe now it makes sense to think that the derivative is an operator. The derivative takes in a function and returns a function. If it were not for convention, I might have used \(\hat{D}\) to denote the derivative as I believe this makes the operator nature of the derivative more apparent. With that said, the key takeaway is to treat \(\odv{}{x}\) as a whole, not as a fraction.


\begin{example}{Notation for derivatives}{}
  The following forms are equal:
  \begin{equation*}
    \odv{f(x)}{x}=\odv{}{x}f(x)
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}


The third notation is used in physics to denote the derivative of a function with respect to time. It is important to note that these notations are interchangeable, and you can use any of them depending on the context.

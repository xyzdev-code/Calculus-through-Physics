\section{Introduction to electrostatics}


Perhaps framing such a well taught subject through the lens of physics is indeed quite unconventional, but I will try my best. I have chosen the realm of electrostatics to begin our deep dive into calculus, and we should slowly progress towards electrodynamics in the later chapters. It is fine if you do not have a deep understanding of the subject matter stated above. (In fact, if you have a complete understanding of these then you obviously have had mastered calculus and more.) For the reason I believe electromagnetism is a great topic to teach calculus in, look at the following equations:

\begin{align}
  \nabla \cdot \vec{E} &= \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} \\
  \nabla \cdot \vec{B} &= 0 \\
  \nabla \times \vec{E} &= -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} \\
  \oint_{\mathcal{P}} \vec{H} \cdot \dd{\vec{l}} &= I_{f_{enc}} + \odv{}{t} \int_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{D} \cdot \dd{a}
\end{align}


These are the famous \emph{Maxwell Equations} which collectively describes how electric and magnetic fields are generated. This together with the Lorentz force law forms the foundation of classical electromagnetism. The Lorentz force law is as such:

\begin{equation}
  \vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v}\times\vec{B}) \label{lorentzforce}
\end{equation}


That is quite a lot to take in. I shall explain some of the symbols you have seen, \(\vec{E}\) and \(\vec{B}\) are the electric and magnetic fields respectively. For the rest, we will get to them as we progress through the book. Currently, only the Lorentz force law seems manageable.\footnote{\(\vec{v}\) is velocity of the object.} Even if you just skimmed through the above equations, you would notice two very important symbols that would be the main focus for the next two parts of the book: \(\int\) and \(\odv{}{t}\). The former is called the \textbf{integral} and the latter is called the \textbf{derivative}. We shall focus on the latter in this part. But first, I shall acquaint you with some prerequisite knowledge regarding electrostatics, the study of electric charges at \emph{rest}. Generally, when dealing with electrostatics here, we will deal with \emph{point charges}.
\begin{definition}
  A \textbf{point charge} is an electric charge that has no spatial size, area or volume.
\end{definition}


An electric charge can be \emph{positive} or \emph{negative}. Like charges \emph{repel} each other and unlike charges \emph{attract} each other. Charge is \emph{quantized}, meaning it comes in integer multiples of an elementary charge, or \(e=1.602176634\times 10^{-19}\si{\coulomb}\).

Notice the lines going in and out of the charges, those are called the \emph{electric field}. For now, we will take it that electric fields originate from charges, but they also can originate from time varying electric currents. 
\begin{definition}
  The \textbf{electric field} is something that associates each point in space with a (force) vector, this kind of mathematical objects are known as \emph{vector fields}. The force will be in the direction of the force on a stationary positive test charge that is placed there. The magnitude or the strength of the field is defined as the force exerted per unit of positive electric charge at that point. We can write that as:
  \begin{equation}
    |\vec{E}| = \frac{F}{q}
  \end{equation}


  where \(F\) is the force acting on the charge and \(q\) is the magnitude of the charge.
\end{definition}


It is probably now a good time to explain the unit system we will be dealing in, SI units. There are other units commonly used for electrodynamics, for example Gaussian units. Formulas are slightly different in both versions and there are many conversion tables you can look up.

As you can see, the positive charge gains velocity as it travels through the region with the electric field. Say the electric field strength is \(5\si{\newton\per\coulomb}\) and the charge is \(2\si{\coulomb}\). Then the charge will experience a force of \(10\si{\newton}\). In fact, we have already seen the first part of the \Cref{lorentzforce}, \(\vec{F}=q\vec{E}\), in action.

Similarly, a negative charge will experience a force opposite to the \(\vec{E}\) field. Notice how the field lines in both figures are equally spaced and parallel, if field lines are illustrated as such, then the electric field is taken to be \emph{uniform}. Since the density of such lines correlate to the field strength, a charge placed in a uniform electric field will experience the same electric force anywhere within this region. We shall introduce the last object in this section: the \textit{test charge}. For the purposes of this text, we will treat them like hypothetical electric charges where all we care is the influence of the electric field on it, and it has no influence on the field that is studied.

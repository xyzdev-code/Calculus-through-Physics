If you are just willing to learn Calc I and II, you do not need the knowledge here. With that said, let's delve into Vectors. A concept that is very ubiquitous in this world is a coordinate, like the GPS coordinate in the maps app. A coordinate simply describes the position of a point in space. Generally, it is denoted \((x,y)\) in 2 dimensions and \((x,y,z)\) in 3 dimensions. Likewise, vectors are also behaves like coordinates and are written down in a \textit{similar} fashion. Think of a vector as a coordinate. \marginnote{\footnotesize \textit{Likewise, a vector at \((x,y,z)\) is denoted \(
    \begin{bsmallmatrix}
      x \\
      y \\
      z
    \end{bsmallmatrix}
\)}}
\begin{definition}{Vector}{}
  A vector is a mathematical object with a magnitude, or length, and a direction. A vector, \(\vec{a}\) is a point \((x,y)\) in space drawn with an arrow. It is denoted as:
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{a} = \begin{bmatrix}
                x \\
                y
              \end{bmatrix} 
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}


Of course, vectors by themselves are not very useful. Hence, we shall define the vector space. For the purposes of this text, you may think of vector spaces as the 2 and 3 dimensional euclidean spaces you are very familiar with. In a vector space, we can finally meaningfully manipulate them. Take a look at the vector \( \vec{a}=
  \begin{bsmallmatrix}
    1 \\
    2
  \end{bsmallmatrix}
  \) and the vector \( \vec{b}=
  \begin{bsmallmatrix}
    -2 \\
    1
  \end{bsmallmatrix}
\) in the two dimensional euclidean space:

You may have noticed that the coordinate axes in \Cref{vecab} are the same as the ones you are familiar with. However, in linear algebra, instead of the usual \(xy\) axes to describe the components of a vector, you have \(\ihat\) and \(\jhat\) to describe the components of a vector.(So instead of saying the "x" component of a vector, you would say the "i hat" component of a vector.) \marginnote{\footnotesize \textit{Some authors use \(\hat{\textbf{x}}, \hat{\textbf{y}}, \hat{\textbf{z}}\) instead of the usual \(\ihat , \jhat , \khat\). We shall use the former.}} This is a convention in linear algebra. \(\ihat\) translates to the \(x\) axis and \(\jhat\) translates to the \(y\) axis. Likewise, in the third dimension, \(\khat\) represents the \(z\) axis. Another thing to note are the \textit{basis vectors}:
\begin{definition}{Basis vectors}{}
  The set of vectors in a vector space are called \textit{basis} if every vector in the vector space can be written uniquely written as a finite \textit{linear combination} \sidenotemark of the basis vectors. An example would be these vectors \(\ihat=\begin{bsmallmatrix}
   0 \\
   1
  \end{bsmallmatrix} \) and \(\jhat=\begin{bsmallmatrix}
   1 \\
   0
  \end{bsmallmatrix} \) in the 2 dimensional euclidean space which can be thought of one unit of distance in the \(x\) and \(y\) direction respectively.
  \label{basis}
\end{definition}
\sidenotetext{\footnotesize \textit{A linear combination of \(x\) and \(y\) is \(ax+by\) where \(a, b\) are constants.}} 
With this in mind, another way of writing a vector \(\vec{a}=
\begin{bsmallmatrix}
  x \\
  y 
\end{bsmallmatrix} \) is \(\vec{a}=x \ihat + y \jhat \). \marginnote{\footnotesize \textit{You can think of \(\ihat\) and \(\jhat\) as one unit of direction in the x and y axis respectively.}} Notice that like in \Cref{basis}, \(\ihat\) and \(\jhat\) are written in a linear combination of \(x\) and \(y\). Of course, these are not the only basis vectors in 2d, if you have learned polar coordinates before, there is the equivalent \(\boldsymbol\hat{\textbf{r}}\) and \(\boldsymbol\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\), but this is a topic for a later chapter. Next, we shall look at the \textit{addition} of vectors. A vector \(\vec{a}=
    \begin{bsmallmatrix}
      x_a \\ 
      y_a
    \end{bsmallmatrix}
  \) added to a vector \(
    \vec{b}=
    \begin{bsmallmatrix}
      x_b \\
      y_b
    \end{bsmallmatrix}
  \) is the vector \(
    \begin{bsmallmatrix}
      x_a + x_b \\
      y_a + y_b
    \end{bsmallmatrix}
    \). As you can see, the addition of two vectors results in another vector. Here is a illustration to help clear up things: \marginnote{\footnotesize \textit{This can be extrapolated into any dimensions: \(\begin{bsmallmatrix}
      a \\
      b \\
      c
    \end{bsmallmatrix}+\begin{bsmallmatrix}
      d \\
      e \\
      f
    \end{bsmallmatrix} = \begin{bsmallmatrix}
      a+d \\
      b+e \\
      c+f
    \end{bsmallmatrix} \).}}

Let us analyze \Cref{vecadd}. Firstly, notice that \(\vec{a}=1 \ihat + 2 \jhat\) and \(\vec{b}=1 \ihat + 1 \jhat\). Thus, \(\vec{a}+\vec{b}=2 \ihat + 3 \jhat\), exactly like in our figure. One way to understand this is to "attach" the vector \(\vec{a}\)'s tail to \(\vec{b}\)'s head by translating \(\vec{a}\).

You may have seen this in your physics class before. Similarly, the subtraction of vectors \(\vec{x}=
\begin{bsmallmatrix}
  a \\
  b
\end{bsmallmatrix}
\) and \(\vec{y}=
  \begin{bsmallmatrix}
    c \\
    d
  \end{bsmallmatrix}
\) is \(\vec{x}-\vec{y}=
\begin{bsmallmatrix}
  a - c \\
  b - d
\end{bsmallmatrix}
\). Of course, we can use the same "head to tail" model to explain vector subtraction. Subtracting \(\vec{y}\) from \(\vec{x}\) is simply finding the vector whose tail is on the head of \(\vec{y}\) and whose head is on the head of \(\vec{x}\). However, some of you may be scratching your heads right now, what if a vector does not start from the \textit{origin} (or the coordinate \((0,0)\)) but its tail lands on, say \((1,1)\). How do we express such a vector? The answer may be surprising, both vectors have the same representation. However, unless specifically stated, \textit{all} vectors start from the origin. This is also why the "head to tail" model works, while the "tail" of the vector is not at the origin, translating it to the origin results in an \textit{identical} vector.

\begin{example}{Addition of vectors}{}
  Here are some examples of vectors:
  \begin{eqnarray*}
    \vec{a} = \begin{bmatrix}
      1 \\
      2
    \end{bmatrix} & 
    \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix}
      -3 \\
      4
    \end{bmatrix} &
    \vec{c} = \begin{bmatrix}
      2 \\
      -2
    \end{bmatrix}
  \end{eqnarray*}
  Here are some examples of adding and subtracting vectors. Note that addition are \textit{commutative}, in other words the order does not matter:
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix}
      1 - 3 \\
      2 + 4
    \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
      -2 \\
      6
    \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{a} - \vec{c} = \begin{bmatrix}
      -1 \\
      4
    \end{bmatrix} 
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{a} + \vec{b} - \vec{c} = \begin{bmatrix}
      -2 \\
      6
    \end{bmatrix} - \vec{c} = \begin{bmatrix}
      -4 \\
      8
    \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}


Given a vector \(\vec{a}\), \(-\vec{a}\) can be thought of the same vector with the same magnitude but pointing in the \textit{opposite} direction. To find it, simply flip the signs of all the components in \(\vec{a}\). If you add \(\vec{a}\) to \(-\vec{a}\), you get what is called the \textit{zero vector}, or the vector \(\begin{bsmallmatrix}
 0 \\
 0
\end{bsmallmatrix}\). This is a vector without direction has zero magnitude. We shall denote it \(\vec{0}\).
Lastly, I want to briefly touch on the idea of \textit{magnitude} which is basically the length of a vector. The derivation of the following formula is simply the Pythagorean theorem.
\begin{definition}{Magnitude of vector}{}
  The magnitude of a vector \(\vec{v}\) in n-dimensional space is as such:
  \begin{equation*}
    ||\vec{v}|| = \sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+...+v_{n}^{2}} 
  \end{equation*}
  A \textit{unit} vector is a vector with magnitude 0.
  \label{vecmagnitude}
\end{definition}
